================================================================================
1. WHAT WAS DONE
================================================================================

Three back ends are compared on the same arc: code written locally, SciPy's
solve_ivp, and tudatpy.

All three run in ONE process and share ONE mesh, ONE polyhedral evaluable and ONE
right-hand side,

    rdot = v
    vdot = R(wt) g(R(-wt) r)

so the only difference between them is the code stepping the ODE. The comparison
is about the integrator and not about the gravity model.

SETUP
  Arc          35 km circular orbit, seed 20260916, 1.5 revolutions,
               tf = 92339.9 s - the same arc as the parking-orbit benchmark
  Frame        inertial, 6 states, no thrust, no mass
  Gravity      Werner-Scheeres polyhedral, Gaskell ver64q, 49 152 facets,
               density set to reproduce GM = 4.4665e5 m^3/s^2, for ALL back ends
  Reference    the benchmark: local RK4 at dt = 32 s

HOW tudat IS DRIVEN
  tudat's own custom_state hook, which is meant to accept an arbitrary
  dy/dt = f(t,x), raises "input type for custom dynamics is inconsistent" in the
  C++ layer for every combination of state shape, time type and body list that was
  tried. The working route is a translational propagator whose single acceleration
  is a Python callback; the callback reads the vehicle's inertial state back out of
  the environment, which tudat updates before each stage. The dynamics integrated
  are therefore identical to the other two back ends, which the RK4 result below
  confirms directly.

WARM-UP
  Every back end is run once on a throwaway 200 s arc before timing. The first
  call into SciPy and into tudat pays import, allocation and JIT costs that belong
  to neither integrator: without the warm-up the first SciPy run measured
  1877 us/evaluation of integration overhead against 9 us for every later one.

MEMOISATION
  Deliberately off. The scvx version of this field caches on the bit pattern of r,
  because polyhedral_gravity 3.3.1 sums its facet contributions in a
  non-deterministic order. That scatter is about 2e-18 relative, roughly
  1e-20 m/s^2, which integrates to ~1e-11 m over this arc - four orders below the
  benchmark's own error. Caching would have corrupted the timing instead, since a
  memo hit returns without touching the field.

================================================================================
3. WHAT THE NUMBERS SAY
================================================================================

THE TWO RK4 RUNS AGREE, WHICH VALIDATES THE WHOLE SETUP
  local  1.727e-05 m over 5772 evaluations
  tudat  1.724e-05 m over 5776 evaluations
  Agreement to 0.2%, and 4.00 evaluations per step on both. The four extra
  evaluations are one extra step from tudat's exact-termination handling of the
  final, trimmed step. Two independently written integrators reaching the same
  answer through the same field is the strongest evidence that the tudat callback
  really is integrating the same dynamics.

THE LIBRARY IS NOT THE COST. THE GRAVITY FIELD IS.
  Integration overhead is 1.9 to 3.0 percent of wall clock for EVERY back end,
  and 9 to 12 us per evaluation against 356 to 396 us for the polyhedral call.
  tudat's C++ to Python boundary crossing costs nothing measurable over SciPy's
  pure-Python stepping. Choosing between these libraries on speed is choosing
  between 97.5% and 97.5%.

  This means the only figure of merit that matters is EVALUATIONS TO REACH AN
  ACCURACY, which is what the plot shows.

AT MATCHED TOLERANCE, SciPy IS MORE ACCURATE PER EVALUATION THAN tudat AT ORDER 5
  SciPy's RK45 is Dormand-Prince; tudat's rkf_45 is Fehlberg. At every tolerance
  tudat spends 1.3 to 1.45 times more evaluations AND ends up less accurate:
  at rtol = 1e-10, SciPy needs 3122 evaluations for 2.96e-5 m where tudat needs
  4356 for 2.67e-4 m. On the work-precision curve SciPy's order-5 line sits below
  and left of tudat's throughout.

AT ORDER 8 THE ORDERING REVERSES
  tudat's rkdp_87 is more accurate per evaluation than SciPy's DOP853 over most of
  the range: 1508 evaluations for 5.56e-5 m against SciPy's 1202 for 1.02e-3 m, and
  2509 for 8.03e-7 m against 2006 for 3.12e-5 m. Reading the curves rather than the
  tolerances, tudat's order-8 line is the best of the four.

  ANOMALY, and it is reproducible: rkdp_87 at rtol = 1e-12 spends 26 806
  evaluations, an eleven-fold jump from 2509 at 1e-10, to land at 1.18e-6 m - no
  better than it managed at 1e-10. The step-size controller is thrashing at that
  tolerance. It is not noise; it repeated across runs. Do not use tudat's default
  step-size control at rtol below about 1e-11 without looking at what it does.

TOLERANCE IS NOT COMPARABLE ACROSS LIBRARIES
  The same rtol means different things to the two libraries, because the error
  estimators and the step controllers differ. Comparing them at matched rtol is
  meaningless; comparing them at matched ACCURACY, by reading across the
  work-precision curves, is the only fair comparison.

================================================================================
5. LIMITS
================================================================================

  - One arc only, an unthrusted 35 km orbit. Behaviour across thrust
    discontinuities is a separate question and is not tested here.
  - The reference is the dt = 32 s benchmark at ~1e-6 m, so the bottom decade of
    the figure is floor-limited and four of the runs sit inside it.
  - tudat's Bulirsch-Stoer and Adams-Bashforth-Moulton were not tested, nor were
    SciPy's Radau, BDF and LSODA. Only the schemes with a counterpart in the other
    library were run, so that every comparison is like for like.
  - Timing is single-core, single-machine, with threading pinned to one thread.
